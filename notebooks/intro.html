Scientific context and scope

Metabolomics in nutritional epidemiology

Metabolomics measures small molecules in biofluids and tissues and provides a high-dimensional readout of endogenous metabolism plus the exogenous inputs (e.g., diet, drugs, pollutants, microbiome-derived metabolites). In nutritional epidemiology, it is increasingly used to: (i) improve dietary exposure assessment, (ii) identify mechanistic pathways linking diet to disease, and (iii) support metabolic phenotyping relevant for precision prevention.

An additional motivation is that self-reported diet instruments (e.g., FFQs, recalls) introduce substantial measurement error because they rely on memory, social desirability, and imperfect reporting (including under- or over-reporting), which can attenuate diet–disease associations and bias inference. Metabolomics has the potential to objectively measure intake, leveraging and/or substituting traditional assessment.

Key study designs in the field:

  • Controlled dietary interventions: strongest for causal attribution and biomarker discovery.

  • Prospective cohorts: strong for disease prediction and mediation pathways (but confounding and reverse causation must be addressed).

  • Nested case-control / case-cohort: efficient for costly metabolomics profiling.

Common analytic challenges:

  • High dimensionality, correlation structures, batch effects, data missingness, and metabolite identification uncertainty.

  • Confounding and reverse causality (especially in cross-sectional settings).

  • Reproducibility and portability across platforms and labs (harmonization).

Exposure biomarkers

Metabolites can reflect:

  • Short-term intake (hours-days): for instance, polyphenol metabolites after intake of products rich in these compounds).

  • Intake patterns (weeks-months): stable patterns that can reflect dietary patterns and macronutrient balance.

A widely used approach is to identify food intake biomarkers from metabolomics-derived molecules and evaluate them on: plausibility and specificity to food, dose-response, intake kinetics, and robustness across populations and platforms. A common practical framework has been to:

  1. Use convential dietary instruments (FFQ or questionnaires) to estimate intake or pattern scores,
  2. Use metabolomics to identify biomarkers or signatures correlated with those exposures,
  3. Validate biomarkers in interventions or independent cohorts,
  4. Use biomarkers to improve the measurement, adherence monitoring and mechanistic inference,
  5. Use these biomarkers in epidemiological analyses to investigater associations with clinical or biological outcomes.

But single metabolites are influenced by multiple factors such as host genetics and physiology, microbiome, physical activity, medication, and disease state, among others. Metabolites are not specific and reflect heterogeneity, therefore there is need for objective biomarkers that capture metabolic adaptation.

Diet and disease research

Metabolomics can support diet research in different ways:

  • Exposure validation and calibration
    Metabolites can help detect questionnaires misreporting, provide calibration signals alongside classical biomarkers and assess adherence to dietary patterns.

  • Pathway discovery and mechanistic interpretation
    Metabolomics can map metabolism shifts (e.g., amino acid metabolism, lipid remodeling, bile acids, microbiome-linked pathways, inflammatory pathways) relevant to disease-related outcomes.

  • Pattern recognition
    Rather than single nutrients, metabolomics can characterize dietary patterns (e.g., Mediterranean Diet, dietary indices) as multivariate signatures.

Metabolic adaptation to diet is shaped by multiple factors, such as baseline physiology and environment, which motivates multi-marker approaches rather than single metabolites to better represent and summarize the metabolic state.

Precision nutrition

Precision nutrition aims to deliver dietary guidance tailored to individuals or stratified subgroups based on biological variability (i.e., genetics or epigenetics, lifestyle, exposome, microbiome, sex, ancestry or psychosocial factors). Because this integrates multiple data layers, it often requires advanced statistical learning approaches. Machine learning approaches could be a way to facilitate this prediction and biomarker discovery in the personalized nutrition context.

In this field, metabolomics provides a tool to estimate intake or pattern scores, characterize individuals metabolic state, identify metabolic subtypes responding differently to diets (metabotypes), monitor adherence and biological response and stratify subgroups of individuals who benefit most from specific dietary strategies.

Multi-metabolite signatures

A multi-metabolite signature (MMS) is a weighted combination of multiple metabolites selected and combined to represent an exposure, phenotype, or biological process (e.g., metabolome-derived diet score).

In practice, MMS are usually derived from:

  • supervised prediction of an exposure (diet score, nutrient intake, pattern adherence),

  • supervised prediction of outcomes (T2D, CVD), interpreted as biological risk signatures,

  • usupervised methods to identify metabolic patterns without a predifined outcome.

Advantages over single metabolites

While single metabolites often lack of specificity and can be noisy, MMS can easily summarize the metabolic complexity and provide a more comprehensive picture of a metabolic state.

Some advantages MMS offer:

  • increase signal-to-noise by aggregating correlated features,

  • better capture complex biological relations and pathways,

  • improve predictive performance and robustness,

  • offer a scalar score for downstream epidemiological analyses.

Methodological landscape for MMS construction

Penalized regression

Metabolomics data typically contain large number of highly correlated predictors relative to the number of observations (high number of metabolites are measured in a often much smaller sample size). This situation is often referred as high dimensionality of the data (p>>n) and supposes a challange for conventional regression approaches.

Standard multiple regression models often perform poorly under these conditions due to overfitting (where the model captures noise instead of the true biological signal), multicollinearity (as metabolites often belong to shared biochemical pathays and therefore have strong correlations), unstable coefficient estimates (where small changes in the data can lead to large changes in the model parameters).

The Penalized regression methods address these challenges by introducing a regularization penalty to the regression model. This penalty constrains the magnitude of the regression coefficients, shrinking them toward zero (i.e., the model is forced to keep the coefficients small, and therefore less influential). As a result, the model becomes more stable, less prone to overfitting and better predicts high-dimensional settings.

LASSO

The Least Absolut Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) applies an L1 penalty to the regression coefficients. This shrinks coefficients to zero, performing variable selection.

LASSO is particularly useful when the goal is to identify a sparse set of predictive metabolites, highlighting those most strongly associated with the exposure or outcome.

⚠️In metabolite datasets where many metabolites are strongly associated, LASSO may arbitrarily select one metabolite from a correlated group while excluding others that might be biologically relevant.

Elastic Net

Elastic net combines L1 (LASSO) and L2 (ridge) penalties, allowing to retain groups of correlated predictors while still performing regularization. This approach is particularly well suited for metabolomics data because metabolites within the same biochemical pathway often exhibit strong correlations.

Elastic Net provides a balance between feature selection and coefficient stabilization, and has become one of the most widely used methods for constructing multi-metabolite signatures in nutritional metabolomics studies.

Ridge Regression

Ridge regression applies an L2 penalty, shrinking coefficients toward zero but not forcing them exactly to zero. As a result, all predictors remain in the model but with reduced magnitude.

This approach is particularly useful when many metabolites contribute small but meaningful signals to a biological process. In such situations, ridge regression can produce more stable and reproducible metabolite signatures, especially when predictors are highly correlated. This is the preferred approach if you want to keep all metabolite predictors in the signature, instead of performing a variable selection.

Dimension reduction

PCA

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is an unsupervised dimension reduction method that transforms high-dimensional metabolomics data into a smaller set of orthogonal components capturing the major sources of variance. It is widely used for exploratory analysis, including visualization, detection of batch effects, and identification of metabolic clustering or phenotypes.

Each principal component represents a weighted combination of metabolites, and component scores can be calculated for each individual. These scores can be interpreted as metabolite patterns, allowing PCA-derived components to function as unsupervised multi-metabolite signatures. Such metabolite patterns can then be examined in relation to dietary exposures or health outcomes.

In nutritional epidemiology, PCA has been applied to identify metabolite patterns associated with dietary habits. For example, PCA has been used to derive metabolite components from diet-related metabolites, which were subsequently associated with specific food groups using multivariable regression models (Kim et al. 2021).

In the context of multi-metabolite signature (MMS) construction, PCA can also serve as a preprocessing step for dimensionality reduction, for instance by reducing hundreds of metabolites into a smaller set of components that can be used as inputs in predictive models. However, because PCA is unsupervised, the derived components do not necessarily align with a specific exposure or outcome of interest, which may limit their utility for biomarker development compared to supervised approaches.

PLS / PLS-DA

Partial Least Squares (PLS) is a supervised dimension reduction method that identifies latent components by maximizing the covariance between metabolite data and a predefined outcome or exposure. Unlike PCA, which captures overall variance, PLS components are explicitly oriented toward explaining variation that is relevant for a specific biological or dietary variable (Fonville et al. 2010). In the context of metabolomics, PLS is commonly used when the goal is to relate high-dimensional metabolite profiles to continuous outcomes, such as dietary intake or biomarker levels (Yang et al. 2017).

PLS-DA (Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis) is an extension of PLS adapted for categorical outcomes, where the objective is to identify metabolite patterns that discriminate between predefined groups (e.g., high vs low adherence to a dietary pattern, or disease vs control). Both PLS and PLS-DA generate components that represent weighted combinations of metabolites, and individual component scores can be interpreted as multi-metabolite signatures associated with the exposure or classification of interest.

In nutritional metabolomics, PLS-based approaches have been used to derive metabolite patterns associated with dietary exposures and to identify discriminatory metabolic profiles linked to dietary patterns or disease risk. Because these components are optimized for prediction, they often provide more direct biological relevance to the exposure compared to unsupervised methods such as PCA.

However, PLS and especially PLS-DA are prone to overfitting, particularly in high-dimensional settings with limited sample sizes. Therefore, rigorous validation strategies (such as cross-validation, permutation testing, and external validation) are essential to ensure robustness. Additionally, the interpretability of PLS-derived metabolite signatures depends on the stability of component loadings, as unstable weights may limit biological interpretation and reproducibility across studies.

Machine learning

Machine-learning methods can be used to construct multi-metabolite signatures when relationships between metabolites and the exposure or outcome may be complex or non-linear. These methods can incorporate a large number of predictors and may capture interactions that are not easily represented by linear regression models.

Random forest

  • Captures non-linearities and interactions. The variable importance can suggest candidate metabolites.

  • Needs carful tuning and validation; importance measures can be biased toward certain feature types.

Gradient boosting

  • Strong predictive performance; handles complex non-linear patterns.

  • Similar caveats: tuning, calibration, and external validation are crucial.

Support vector machines

  • Effective in high-dimensional settings; kernel choice controls non-linearity.

  • Less straightforward interpretability; works best with standardized features and tuned hyperparameters.

Scoring frameworks

After selecting the metabolites and estimating their contribution, the information must be combined into a score that can be calculated for each participant. The resulting score summarizes the metabolite profile into a single variable that can be used in validation and downstream epidemiological analyses.

Weighted scores

A weighted multi-metabolite score is commonly calculated by multiplying each metabolite value by its corresponding model coefficient and then summing the resulting values:

[ MMS_i = _{j=1}^{p} j X*{ij}]

where (X_{ij}) is the standardized value of metabolite (j) for participant (i), and (_j) is the weight estimated by the model.

Metabolites with larger coefficients contribute more strongly to the score. Positive and negative coefficients indicate the direction of their contribution.

Metabolite risk scores

A metabolite risk score combines several metabolites associated with a disease outcome or biological risk factor. It is conceptually similar to other composite risk scores, but it uses metabolite concentrations instead of genetic variants or clinical variables.

The score can be examined in relation to disease incidence, clinical biomarkers or other health outcomes. Its predictive value should preferably be evaluated beyond established demographic, lifestyle and clinical risk factors.

Reporting and reproducibility expectations

Clear reporting is important to ensure that a multi-metabolite signature can be understood, reproduced and evaluated in other studies. Ideally, it should include:

  • the study population and sample size,

  • metabolomics measurement and quality-control procedures,

  • variables preprocessing, transformation and standardization;

  • handling of missing values and batch effects,

  • the definition of the exposure or outcome,

  • the method used to divide the data into training and validation sets,

  • the model, tuning parameters and cross-validation procedure,

  • the metabolites included in the final signature and their weights,

  • scores and score calculatoin,

  • measures of model performance and uncertainty,

  • the software and packages used in the analysis.

When possible, the analysis code and sufficient information for calculating the signature should be made available. External validation in an independent population is also recommended to assess whether the signature is reproducible and transferable across studies, populations and metabolomics platforms.
